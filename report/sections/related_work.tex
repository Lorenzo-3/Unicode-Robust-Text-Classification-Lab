\section{Related work}
\subsection{From visible substitutions to encoding attacks}
Eger et al.~\cite{eger2019visual} studied visual character substitutions across character, word and sentence level NLP tasks. Both neural and non-neural systems were sensitive to changes that human readers could often interpret. The three shielding approaches they tested were visual character embeddings, adversarial training and rule-based recovery. Those defenses improved robustness but did not fully restore clean performance.

Boucher et al.~\cite{boucher2022bad} broadened the problem to encoding-level attacks: invisible characters, homoglyphs, reordering controls and deletion controls. They demonstrated black-box failures across several NLP applications, including deployed systems. I studied a narrower setting with fixed perturbation budgets and the same attacked examples for every classifier. Reordering and deletion controls are outside this experiment.

\subsection{Why tokenization matters}
Geh et al.~\cite{geh2025adversarial} investigated a different interface. They selected alternative valid tokenizations of an unchanged string to bypass language-model safety restrictions. Here, the tokenizer is fixed and the Unicode input changes. These attacks should not be treated as equivalent, but both make the token sequence relevant to robustness.

\subsection{What a repair method can recover}
Cooper et al.~\cite{cooper2025lies} used language models to normalize homoglyph-obfuscated tweets, either by directly replacing lookalikes or by reconstructing text after non-Latin characters were masked. Their approach can address substitutions beyond a fixed repair dictionary.
